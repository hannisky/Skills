## Understanding Zone 4

Zone 4 in Scandinavia means winter lows between **−34 °C and −29 °C**, with a frost-free growing season of roughly **4 months** (late May to late September). Key implications:

- **Short season:** Choose early- to mid-season varieties for every crop. Late-season varieties often won't mature.
- **Frost hardiness matters:** Brassicas, root vegetables, and alliums are well suited. Frost-tender crops (tomatoes, peppers, squash) require protected growing environments not included in this plan.
- **Light advantage:** Long summer days (18–22 hours of light at these latitudes) accelerate vegetative growth significantly. Plants grow faster here than the same varieties at lower latitudes.
- **Soil temperature lag:** Even after air temperatures warm, clay soil stays cold. Don't rush spring planting — use a soil thermometer and wait for 7 °C at 10 cm depth before sowing root crops.

## Soil Management for Heavy Clay

This site has extreme clay soil. Clay retains moisture and nutrients well but presents serious challenges:

### Problems
- **Compaction:** Foot traffic or machinery on wet clay destroys structure for an entire season.
- **Waterlogging:** Clay drains slowly. Roots can suffocate in saturated soil.
- **Crusting:** Bare clay surfaces dry into a hard crust that blocks seedling emergence.
- **Cold:** Clay warms slowly in spring, delaying planting.

### The Strategy: Build From the Top
Rather than fighting the clay, the plan works *on top of it*:

1. **Never dig wet clay.** Use the squeeze test: take a handful of soil and squeeze. If it forms a sticky, shiny ball, it's too wet. Wait until it crumbles.
2. **Topdress annually.** Apply 3–5 cm of compost and well-rotted manure as a surface layer every spring. Don't work it in deeply — let earthworms pull it down. Over years, this builds a rich topsoil horizon.
3. **Mulch everything.** Paths get hay (5–10 cm). Beds get straw or leaf mulch after harvest. Mulch suppresses weeds, retains moisture in summer, and protects soil structure from rain impact.
4. **Fork, don't turn.** Use a broadfork or garden fork to loosen soil to 20–25 cm without inverting layers. Inverting clay buries topsoil organisms and brings up dead subsoil.
5. **Permanent beds.** Never walk on growing beds. The 2 m central path and bed layout are designed so every part of every bed is reachable from the path or edge.

### Long-Term Improvement
Heavy clay can become excellent garden soil over 3–5 years of consistent organic matter addition. Each year, the worm population grows, drainage improves, and the soil becomes darker and more friable. Patience is the key input.

## Companion Planting

Companion planting uses the relationships between plants to improve pest control, pollination, and space use. Relevant pairings for this garden:

| Pairing | Benefit |
|---|---|
| **Carrots + Alliums** | Allium scent (shallots, garlic) confuses carrot fly; carrot fly is drawn to smell. Root Bed B sits adjacent to the Allium Bed on the opposite side of the path — not ideal for maximum effect but still helpful. |
| **Brassicas + Dill or Chamomile** | Attract beneficial insects that prey on cabbage pests. Consider adding a small dill patch to the Brassica Bed edge in future seasons. |
| **Potatoes + Horseradish** | Horseradish at bed corners can deter Colorado beetle. A long-term addition if beetle pressure appears. |
| **Spinach / Lettuce under Brassicas** | Shade from brassica foliage reduces bolting in greens. The greens trial in the Brassica Bed naturally benefits from this. |

### Antagonistic Pairings (avoid)
- **Alliums near legumes:** Alliums can inhibit legume growth. When legumes are introduced in future rotations, keep them away from the Allium Bed.
- **Potatoes near tomatoes:** Same family (Solanaceae), same diseases. Not relevant this year since tomatoes are excluded.

## Pest & Disease Reference

### Carrot Fly (*Psila rosae*)
- **Threat to:** Root Bed B (carrots, parsnips).
- **Behaviour:** Low-flying pest (below 60 cm). Lays eggs at stem base; larvae tunnel into roots.
- **Control:** Fine-mesh netting (mesh < 2 mm) installed immediately after sowing. Netting must be sealed at all edges. Allium scent from the adjacent Allium Bed provides secondary deterrence.
- **Timing:** Two generations — May–June and August–September.

### Cabbage White Butterfly (*Pieris brassicae*)
- **Threat to:** Brassica Bed (all brassicas).
- **Behaviour:** Lays eggs on leaf undersides. Caterpillars devour leaves rapidly.
- **Control:** Insect netting over the entire Brassica Bed from transplanting onward. Check under nets weekly for any caterpillars that slipped through. Hand-pick if found.
- **Organic treatment:** *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) spray is effective and targeted if netting alone is insufficient.

### Flea Beetle (*Phyllotreta* spp.)
- **Threat to:** Brassica Bed (especially young transplants), rocket in trellis beds.
- **Behaviour:** Tiny jumping beetles that create "shot-hole" damage in leaves.
- **Control:** Netting with fine mesh. Keep soil moist — flea beetles prefer dry conditions. Strong, fast-growing plants outpace damage.

### Colorado Potato Beetle (*Leptinotarsa decemlineata*)
- **Threat to:** Root Bed A (potatoes).
- **Behaviour:** Adults and larvae strip potato foliage. Check northern Scandinavian pest maps — not present everywhere.
- **Control:** Netting if the pest is locally established. Otherwise, hand-pick adults and egg clusters (bright orange, on leaf undersides). Crop rotation reduces overwintering populations.

### Late Blight (*Phytophthora infestans*)
- **Threat to:** Root Bed A (potatoes).
- **Behaviour:** Fungal disease spreading in warm, humid weather (late summer). Brown lesions on leaves, tuber rot.
- **Control:** Choose early-maturing, blight-tolerant varieties. Harvest before September if blight is spotted. Remove and destroy (do not compost) affected foliage immediately.

### Clubroot (*Plasmodiophora brassicae*)
- **Threat to:** Brassica Bed (all brassicas).
- **Behaviour:** Soil-borne pathogen causing swollen, distorted roots. Plants wilt and yellow.
- **Control:** Strict crop rotation (brassicas should not return to the same bed for 3+ years in affected soils). Maintain soil pH above 7.0 if clubroot is present (lime application). Do not move soil from infected areas.

## Garlic: Spring vs. Autumn Planting

### This Year (2026): Spring Planting
Garlic sprouts were started indoors and are already up. They will be planted out in late May with the shallots. Spring-planted garlic will produce usable bulbs, but they will be **noticeably smaller** than autumn-planted garlic. This is a known trade-off for the first season.

### Future Seasons: Autumn Planting (Recommended)
For 2027 onward, garlic should be planted in **October**, 4–6 weeks before the ground freezes:

1. **Why autumn?** Garlic needs a cold period (vernalisation) to trigger bulb division. Autumn-planted cloves root in before winter, survive under snow, and emerge very early in spring with a significant head start.
2. **Bulb size:** Autumn-planted garlic produces bulbs 2–3× larger than spring-planted.
3. **Variety choice:** Hardneck garlic is preferred for Zone 4. It's hardier, produces scapes (edible flower stems — a bonus harvest), and stores well.
4. **Planting depth:** Set cloves 5–8 cm deep, pointy end up, 15 cm apart. Mulch with 10 cm straw immediately after planting.
5. **Calendar entry:** Plant 2027 garlic in October 2026 (already noted in [calendar-2026.md](calendar-2026.md) and [tasks.md](tasks.md)).

## Watering Guidelines

This plan uses manual hose watering. Irrigation infrastructure will be reassessed after the first season based on actual water needs and labour.

### General Principles

- **Water deeply, less often.** A thorough soak every 3–5 days is better than a light sprinkle daily. Deep watering encourages roots to grow downward, improving drought resilience.
- **Water at soil level.** Avoid wetting foliage — this promotes fungal diseases. Direct the hose at the base of plants.
- **Morning watering is ideal.** Leaves dry quickly in morning sun; evening watering leaves foliage damp overnight.
- **Clay soil caveat:** Clay retains moisture much longer than sandy soil. Check moisture at 5 cm depth with a finger before watering — if still damp, wait.

### Crop-Specific Needs

| Crop | Water needs | Critical period |
|---|---|---|
| Potatoes | Moderate | During flowering (tuber formation) |
| Carrots / parsnips | Even, consistent | Germination (2–3 weeks); avoid fluctuations that cause splitting |
| Brassicas | High | Transplanting establishment; head formation |
| Alliums | Low–moderate | Reduce watering as tops yellow (curing phase) |
| Trellis beds | Moderate | Small volume — lids retain moisture; check every 2–3 days |

### Dry Spell Protocol
If no rain for 5+ days during active growing season (June–August):
1. Water Root Beds and Brassica Bed deeply (soak to 15 cm).
2. Water Allium Bed lightly unless plants show stress.
3. Check trellis beds daily — small volume dries faster.
4. Top up path mulch if it looks thin — exposed clay cracks and pulls moisture from bed edges.

## Winter Preparation & Cover Cropping

### End-of-Season Steps (October–November)

1. **Harvest everything** that won't survive frost (except parsnips and kale, which benefit from it).
2. **Remove all netting** and supports. Clean, dry, and store indoors.
3. **Topdress** all beds with 2–3 cm compost.
4. **Apply winter mulch:** 5–10 cm of straw or leaves over every bed. This protects soil biology, prevents erosion, and adds organic matter.
5. **Plant garlic** for next year (October — see garlic section above).

### Cover Cropping (Future Consideration)

Cover crops (green manure) are planted after harvest to protect soil and add nutrients. They're not included in the 2026 plan (first season, keep it simple) but are a strong addition from 2027:

| Cover crop | When to sow | Benefit | Kill method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winter rye | August–September | Erosion control, weed suppression | Cut in spring, leave as mulch |
| Crimson clover | August | Nitrogen fixation (legume) | Winter-kills in Zone 4 |
| Phacelia | July–August | Pollinator magnet, fast biomass | Frost-killed |

## Agroforestry Context & Long-Term Vision

This vegetable garden is the first productive element on a larger property with plans to develop toward **agroforestry** — an integrated land-use system combining trees, shrubs, and annual crops.

### How This Garden Fits

- **Soil building starts now.** The heavy clay soil improves with every season of organic matter addition. By the time tree crops are introduced, the soil biology will be far more active.
- **Crop selection is transferable.** Hardy perennials suited to Zone 4 agroforestry (see below) share space well with the annual crops in this plan.
- **Rotation prepares the ground.** Introducing legumes in year two begins nitrogen fixation, which benefits both annual beds and future tree/shrub underplantings.

### Potential Future Perennials (Zone 4 Hardy)

| Plant | Type | Role in agroforestry |
|---|---|---|
| Sea buckthorn (*Hippophae rhamnoides*) | Shrub | Nitrogen fixer, windbreak, berry production |
| Currants (black, red) | Shrub | Fruit production, low maintenance |
| Rhubarb | Perennial herb | Extremely hardy, high yield, zero pest pressure |
| Haskap / honeyberry (*Lonicera caerulea*) | Shrub | Very early berry, thrives in Zone 4 |
| Apple (hardy rootstock) | Tree | Long-term fruit; needs 5+ years to produce |
| Siberian pea shrub (*Caragana arborescens*) | Shrub/small tree | Nitrogen fixer, windbreak, edible seeds |

### Fence-Line Opportunities

The west fence line is a natural candidate for early perennial plantings (currants, rhubarb, sea buckthorn) that do not interfere with annual bed rotation. These could be introduced as early as autumn 2026 if scope allows.

> The vegetable garden and any future agroforestry elements should be planned together. As perennial elements are added, this reference guide will expand to cover their integration.
